3.1.1 Computational Thinking and AI

Key PointsScriptExample
• Definition of computational thinking<br>• Core concepts: decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, algorithms<br>• Relevance to AI systems<br>• Integrating computational thinking in various subjects"Computational thinking is a problem-solving approach that involves breaking down complex problems, recognizing patterns, creating abstractions, and developing step-by-step solutions. These skills are fundamental to understanding and working with AI systems. By fostering computational thinking across subjects, we're not just preparing students for coding, but equipping them with valuable problem-solving skills applicable to AI and beyond."In a history class, have students apply computational thinking to analyze historical events. For example, they could break down the causes of World War II (decomposition), identify patterns in how conflicts escalate, create an abstraction of key factors leading to war, and develop an algorithm for predicting potential future conflicts. This exercise demonstrates how computational thinking applies beyond computer science and relates to AI's approach to complex problem-solving.

3.1.2 Problem-Solving Strategies for AI Challenges

Key PointsScriptExample
• Common AI challenges in education<br>• Structured problem-solving approaches<br>• Collaborative problem-solving techniques<br>• Iterative design and testing"Implementing AI in education comes with unique challenges, from ensuring fairness to maintaining student engagement. Effective problem-solving strategies are crucial. These include clearly defining the problem, brainstorming multiple solutions, evaluating options, implementing the best approach, and continuously refining based on feedback. Collaborative problem-solving, involving diverse perspectives, is particularly valuable when addressing complex AI-related issues."Create a scenario where an AI-powered tutoring system is showing bias against certain student groups. Guide students through a problem-solving process: Define the problem (bias in the AI system), brainstorm potential causes (biased training data, flawed algorithms), propose solutions (diversifying data, adjusting algorithms), and design a plan to implement and test the chosen solution. This exercise helps students apply structured problem-solving to real-world AI challenges.

3.1.3 Building Data Literacy for AI

Key PointsScriptExample
• Importance of data literacy in AI era<br>• Key data literacy skills: collecting, analyzing, interpreting data<br>• Critical evaluation of data sources and quality<br>• Ethical considerations in data use"Data literacy is crucial in the age of AI. It involves the ability to collect, analyze, and interpret data, as well as critically evaluate data sources and quality. In the context of AI, data literacy helps students understand how AI systems make decisions based on data. It also empowers them to question and validate AI outputs. Building these skills across subjects prepares students to be informed consumers and potential creators of AI technologies."In a science class, conduct a project where students collect weather data over time, use simple machine learning tools to make predictions, and then critically evaluate the accuracy of these predictions. This hands-on experience builds data literacy skills while introducing basic AI concepts.

3.1.4 AI Tools and Platforms for PreK-16 Classrooms

Key PointsScriptExample
• Overview of age-appropriate AI tools<br>• Integration across various subjects<br>• Hands-on learning opportunities<br>• Balancing AI use with traditional methods"A wide range of AI tools and platforms are available for integration into PreK-16 curricula. These tools can enhance learning across subjects, from language arts to STEM fields. When selecting AI tools, consider age-appropriateness, alignment with learning objectives, and opportunities for hands-on engagement. Remember, the goal is to use AI to augment and enhance learning, not replace traditional teaching methods."In an elementary art class, introduce students to AI-powered drawing tools like AutoDraw. Have them start a drawing and see how the AI suggests completions. Discuss how the AI makes these suggestions and how it might influence creativity. This activity introduces young students to AI concepts while engaging them in a familiar, creative task.

3.1.5 Evaluating AI Resources for Educational Use

Key PointsScriptExample
• Criteria for evaluating AI resources<br>• Assessing age-appropriateness and accuracy<br>• Alignment with learning objectives<br>• Considering privacy and ethical implications"When selecting AI resources for your classroom, it's crucial to evaluate them carefully. Key considerations include age-appropriateness, accuracy of information or outputs, alignment with your learning objectives, and privacy and ethical implications. It's also important to assess how the AI resource complements your teaching style and curriculum. Remember, the best AI tool is one that enhances learning while aligning with your educational goals and ethical standards."Create a rubric for evaluating AI-powered educational apps. Include criteria such as accuracy, user interface, alignment with curriculum standards, data privacy policies, and potential for bias. Have teachers use this rubric to assess a selection of AI apps relevant to their subject areas, discussing their findings and recommendations as a group. This exercise provides practical experience in critically evaluating AI resources for educational use.
