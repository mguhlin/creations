Mod 2.1.1 Slide Content

Vignette

Mr. Rodriguez's 10th-grade computer science class buzzed with excitement as they gathered around a large screen. "Today, we're going to explore AI bias," he announced, pulling up a facial recognition app. The students watched in surprise as the app struggled to identify their classmate Aisha's features accurately.

"Why isn't it working for Aisha?" asked Carlos, frowning.

Mr. Rodriguez explained, "This is an example of AI bias. The system wasn't trained on a diverse enough dataset, so it has difficulty recognizing certain facial features."

The class spent the next hour testing the app with various faces, noting discrepancies in its performance across different ethnicities and genders. By the end of the session, the students were engrossed in a lively discussion about the real-world implications of biased AI systems and brainstorming ways to make them fairer.

"Remember," Mr. Rodriguez concluded, "as future developers and users of AI, you have the power to shape these technologies to be more inclusive and equitable."

Thinking Ahead

How can we integrate discussions about AI bias and fairness across various subjects, helping students understand its impact beyond just computer science classes? What real-world examples can we use to make the concept of AI bias more tangible and relevant to students' everyday lives?

Slide 1: Understanding AI Bias and Fairness

Definition: AI bias refers to systematic and unfair discrimination in the outputs or decisions of AI systems, while fairness in AI aims to ensure equitable treatment across all demographics.

Physical Example: Analyzing Facial Recognition Bias

  • Example: Students examine a real-world case where facial recognition systems perform poorly on certain demographics.
  • Explanation: By analyzing this case, students can directly observe how AI bias manifests in practical applications. They can discuss the potential consequences of such bias, such as misidentification or unequal access to services, and brainstorm solutions to improve the system's fairness. This hands-on approach helps students understand the real-world implications of AI bias and the importance of addressing it.

---

Slide 2: Sources of Bias in AI Systems

Definition: Bias in AI can originate from various sources throughout the development and deployment process of AI systems.

Physical Example: Data Exploration Activity

  • Example: Students analyze datasets used for training AI models, identifying potential biases in data collection or representation.
  • Explanation: Through this activity, students gain firsthand experience in recognizing how biased training data can lead to biased AI outputs. They might discover underrepresentation of certain groups or skewed data distributions. This exercise helps students understand the critical role of diverse and representative datasets in creating fair AI systems, aligning with the strategy of focusing on data quality to mitigate bias.

---

Slide 3: Impact of Biased AI on Society

Definition: Biased AI systems can perpetuate and amplify existing societal inequalities, affecting various aspects of people's lives.

Physical Example: AI Decision-Making Simulation

  • Example: Students participate in a role-playing activity where they experience the effects of biased AI decisions in scenarios like job applications or loan approvals.
  • Explanation: This simulation allows students to personally experience the potential consequences of AI bias. By taking on different roles and seeing how biased AI decisions can affect opportunities and outcomes, students develop a deeper understanding of the societal impact of AI bias. This activity encourages empathy and underscores the importance of fairness in AI systems.

---

Slide 4: Strategies for Mitigating AI Bias

Definition: Mitigating AI bias involves implementing various strategies throughout the AI development lifecycle to ensure fairness and reduce discrimination.

Physical Example: Developing Fairness-Aware Algorithms

  • Example: Students work on a project to modify a simple classification algorithm to incorporate fairness constraints.
  • Explanation: In this hands-on coding exercise, students learn how to implement fairness-aware algorithms. They might experiment with techniques like reweighting samples or adjusting decision thresholds to achieve more equitable outcomes across different groups. This activity not only teaches technical skills but also reinforces the concept that bias mitigation is an active process requiring thoughtful intervention.

---

Key Points to Remember:

  • AI bias can lead to unfair discrimination and perpetuate societal inequalities.
  • Sources of bias include biased training data, flawed algorithms, and developer biases.
  • Mitigating bias requires diverse datasets, fairness-aware algorithms, and continuous monitoring.

How can we ensure that AI systems in our schools and communities are fair and unbiased, and what role can students play in this process?
