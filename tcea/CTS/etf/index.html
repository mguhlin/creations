Exploring EdTech Frameworks

As an educator, it helps to have several lenses for observing and assessing how technology transforms and enhances teaching, learning, and leading.

These frameworks serve as aids to campus administrators who may not know what to look for when technology is present in a classroom. For curriculum staff and teachers, each offers a different way of understanding how technology scaffolds instruction.

This is the companion reference to the PATI session on planning and assessing technology integration. Read the companion blog entries by Miguel Guhlin.

▸

Three Ways to Assess Technology

One useful idea up front: you do not have to tie every assessment of technology to student achievement. Some technology earns its place by making professional work, teaching and leading, more effective and productive. With that in mind, here are three approaches.

Approach 1 Technology Audit

Before launching anything that touches every student and staff member, a one-to-one rollout, bring-your-own-technology, a new platform, confirm the infrastructure is ready and the ecosystems match. The most expensive surprise, aside from shifting human habits, is discovering mid-launch that the network cannot carry the vision.

Learn more about technology audits.

Approach 2 Climate and Readiness Surveys

Survey tools gather data from teachers, students, and leaders, then turn it into role-specific reports and recommendations. Used well, they help leaders direct resources where they will have the most impact and track change over time.

Approach 3 Education Frameworks

Frameworks give you a structured lens for what good integration looks like. They divide into theoretical frameworks, which are built to test, predict, and assess across two continuums of growth, and conceptual frameworks, which help practitioners talk about technology but are weaker for assessment. The pages below walk through each.

▸

Theoretical Frameworks

Theoretical frameworks are used to test theories and to assess technology use in schools. They usually track at least two continuums of growth: one for the student, whose role shifts toward creator and problem-solver, and one for how technology moves from productivity toward collaboration and creation.

T3

Sorts technology use as translational, transformational, or transcendent to reach high-impact usage.

HEAT

Higher-order thinking, Engaged learning, Authentic connections, and Technology use.

LoTi

Levels of Teaching Innovation: seven stages from non-use to refinement.

TIM

The Technology Integration Matrix: five characteristics by five levels, 25 cells.

TPACK

The overlap of technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge.

Triple E

Does the tool help students Engage, Enhance, and Extend the learning goals?

PAGER

A teacher-friendly implementation model used for digital badging.
▸

Conceptual Frameworks

Conceptual frameworks aim to develop theory useful to classroom teachers. They are often misused to assess technology use, and they tend to focus on the technology itself rather than on evidence-based instructional strategies. In these models, technology is the protagonist.

SAMR

Substitution, Augmentation, Modification, Redefinition. Widely used, conceptual rather than research-validated.

RAT & PICRAT

Replacement, Amplification, Transformation, plus the student-centered PICRAT extension.

Choosing a framework. You do not need all of these. Pick one theoretical framework as your shared language for walkthroughs and goal-setting, and use it consistently. The discipline of using one lens well beats sampling many.
