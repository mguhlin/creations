Part 4

Perspectives: Assessing Integration

"There is WiFi on Mt. Everest. There are zero excuses for our students to not have WiFi access in their schools and homes." — Tom Murray

Educators benefit from having several lenses for observing and assessing how technology is used to enhance teaching, learning, and leading. These frameworks help campus leaders know what to look for when technology is present, and they give curriculum staff and teachers a shared language for how technology scaffolds instruction.

▸

Three Ways to Assess

One useful suggestion: do not tie every assessment of technology directly to student achievement. Some technology earns its place by making professional work, teaching and leading, more effective and more productive for adults and students alike. With that in mind, here are three approaches.

Approach 1 Technology Audit

Before launching anything that touches every student and staff member, a one-to-one rollout, bring-your-own-technology, a new platform, make sure the infrastructure is ready. The most expensive surprise, aside from shifting human habits, is discovering mid-launch that the network cannot carry the vision. Confirm the ecosystems match before you begin.

Learn more about technology audits.

Approach 2 Climate and Readiness Surveys

Survey-based tools gather data from teachers, students, and leaders, then turn it into role-specific reports and recommendations. Used well, they help leaders direct technology resources where they will have the most impact and track change over time.

Tools in this space change often. Look for one that produces role-specific reports and ties results to clear next steps.

Approach 3 Education Frameworks

Frameworks give you a structured lens for what good integration looks like. They fall roughly into research-based and non-research-based groups. The next sections walk through the most common ones.

▸

The T3 Framework

Sonny Magana's T3 Framework is designed to make technology integration more precise. It helps teachers and leaders self-assess current uses, set growth goals, and reach higher levels of mastery across three domains: translational, transformational, and transcendent uses of technology.

There have been 161 meta-analyses on computers in education, from over ten thousand studies, and the average effect is about d = 0.34, an effect that has not changed much in fifty years despite enormous change in the technology. A major reason is that most technological interventions do not change the dominant "tell and practice" teaching model. Moving to transcendent uses, students solving problems, explaining their thinking to others, and turning classroom monologue into dialogue, is clearly where we should go. This is how we will see technology make the difference we are after. — John Hattie, on Magana's work

The takeaway: the effect of technology depends almost entirely on whether it changes the pedagogy. A framework that pushes you toward transformation and transcendence is doing real work.

▸

Other Research-Based Frameworks

HEAT

Higher-order thinking, Engaged learning, Authentic connections, and Technology use. A walkthrough lens for judging the depth of a lesson, not just the presence of a device.

LoTi

Levels of Teaching Innovation. Describes a progression from awareness through to refinement, focused on how fully technology supports student-centered, inquiry-driven learning.

PAGER

A model for examining how technology supports the phases of a learning task, from preparation through to results and reflection.

Technology Integration Matrix

The TIM crosses five characteristics of meaningful learning with five levels of integration, giving a 25-cell map with classroom video for each cell.

Triple E Framework

The Triple E Framework asks whether a tool helps students Engage in, Enhance, and Extend the learning goals, with a practical scoring rubric.

TPACK

TPACK describes the overlap of technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge. Strong integration lives where all three meet, not in any one alone.

▸

A Widely Used, Non-Research-Based Framework

SAMR

SAMR, Substitution, Augmentation, Modification, and Redefinition, is one of the most recognized models for thinking about technology use, describing a ladder from substituting for an analog tool to redefining a task in ways not otherwise possible.

It is included here with a note: SAMR is a useful conversation starter, but it is a conceptual model rather than a research-validated one. Pair it with a research-based framework such as Triple E or TIM when you need to make decisions or judge impact.

Choosing a framework. You do not need all of these. Pick one research-based framework as your shared language for walkthroughs and goal-setting, and use it consistently. The discipline of using one lens well beats sampling many.
