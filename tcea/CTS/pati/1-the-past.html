Part 1

The Past: Where We Have Been

"What are we here to do? What is the best alignment between what we have to offer, and what people need?" — Irene Au

Educational technology has been around for many years, and many new technologies have arrived in schools. There has been real debate about their effect on student achievement, but no one doubts how engaged educators and students have been. That counts for a lot.

▸

A Timeline of EdTech

Educational technology stretches back decades. These milestones trace the path. For a fuller version, see EDUCAUSE's Twenty Years of EdTech.

1998 — Wikis

A web page anyone could jointly edit. The wiki turned publishing into a shared, collaborative enterprise.

1999 — E-Learning

The term had been around, but the rise of the web and the "e" prefix pushed it into common use.

2000 — Learning Objects

A digitized entity that could be used, reused, or referenced during technology-supported learning.

2001 — E-Learning Standards

Bodies like IMS set out to describe content, assessment, and courses. SCORM became the standard for content in virtual learning environments.

2002 — Open Educational Resources

MIT's OpenCourseWare and the new Creative Commons licenses let people share resources easily. OER became a global movement.

2003 — Blogs

Once people realized anyone could publish on the web, journals and regularly updated resources followed. RSS let readers subscribe and stay current.

2004 — Learning & Course Management Systems

The LMS offered an enterprise home for e-learning, pulling together communication, content, and pages that had lived in separate tools.

2005 — YouTube & Video Sharing

Anyone could make and share a video. As Khan Academy showed, short explanations of key concepts met real demand.

2006 — Web 2.0

A useful label for user-generated services like YouTube, Flickr, and blogs, and for a new mindset about our relationship to the internet.

2007 — Second Life & Virtual Worlds

Colleges built islands and ran whole courses inside virtual worlds.

2008 — ePortfolios

A place to gather evidence of formal and informal learning. Like learning objects, it never became the standard form of assessment some predicted.

2009 — Twitter & Social Media

Global connections, cross-disciplinary conversation, and a democratizing effect where contributions mattered more than formal status.

2010 — Connectivism

Proposed by George Siemens and Stephen Downes, arguably the first internet-native learning theory: learning across nebulous, shifting networks.

2011 — Personal Learning Environments

Instead of one LMS for all, learners assembled their own blend of tools, and the question became how to glue them together.

2012 — MOOCs

The year of the massive open online course, combining OER openness, video, connectivism, and Web 2.0 hype.

2013 — Open Textbooks

Openly licensed textbooks, free in digital form, shifted cost and offered an easy route to adoption.

2014 — Learning Analytics

Attention turned to what students actually do once they are logged into a system.

2015 — Digital Badges

Building on Mozilla's open badge infrastructure, badges tied achievements to verifiable evidence, with the usual slow climb to wide acceptance.

2016 — The Return of Artificial Intelligence

The worry was never that AI would fail to deliver, but that one day it would, and that assumptions baked into code would shape how education works for every learner who does not fit the model.

Where the timeline points now. Each wave above promised transformation and delivered something more modest and more useful. Generative AI is the current wave. The lesson from twenty-plus years of edtech is to ask what problem a tool solves before adopting it, a theme that runs through the rest of this session.
