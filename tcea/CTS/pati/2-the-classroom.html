Part 2

The Classroom: Achieving the Ideal

"The technology we introduce must serve a purpose, and that purpose must be to enhance learning through improving pedagogy." — Dr. Michael Cowling

What should technology integration look like in the classroom? Several models can shape how a classroom works. Here are the ones you are most likely to encounter, each with a brief description and a place to learn more.

Model 1 Project-Based Learning

Project-based learning remains one of the most popular ways to blend technology into the curriculum. Related approaches, including problem-based learning and inquiry-based learning, have also found their way into schools.

There are many free resources available. A good starting point is PBLWorks, which offers a research-backed project design framework and a library of classroom-ready projects.

Model 2 Constructivist Approach

Constructivism treats learning as an active, constructive process. The learner builds knowledge, connecting new information to prior knowledge so that mental representations are personal and subjective.

WebQuests were one popular constructivist approach, and with a little extra work they doubled as project-based learning. A durable organizing idea for them is the cycle of inputs, transformations, and outputs: students gather sources, do something with them, and produce something new.

Background: Constructivism (Learning Theories).

Model 3 One-to-One Devices

With Chromebooks, iPads, and Windows tablets widely available, one-to-one programs are common. A Texas study of one-to-one laptops found that students with greater access and use, especially for learning outside school, scored significantly higher in reading and mathematics. Home learning was the strongest single predictor.

The same research is a caution as much as an endorsement. Access alone is not the lever; how, and how often, students use the device for real learning is what moved scores. One-to-one programs have also produced expensive, well-documented failures when planning and purpose were thin.

Model 4 STEM, STEAM, and Robotics

STEM education, science, technology, engineering, and mathematics, draws steady attention. Jobs in these fields are plentiful, yet student interest has not kept pace with demand, and the gap is widest for women and students of color.

TCEA Robotics resources Read the TCEA blog on STEM

Note: the statewide TCEA robotics contest has been retired, but TCEA continues to publish problem sets and resources that any classroom, campus, or district can use to run its own contest.

Model 5 The Digital Campus

A digital campus describes the conditions that let integration happen. A sample profile includes:

  • Classroom access. Each teacher has a personal computing device; students and staff have managed accounts; classrooms have a display, audio, and access to web-based and online materials.
  • Network connectivity. High-speed wired drops in each room, wireless access points sized for the devices in use, and guest access for visitors and bring-your-own-technology.
  • Campus access. Shared device carts sized to campus needs, plus the ability to run announcements and broadcasts over the network.
  • Professional learning. Campus improvement plans reflect ongoing learning in blended and flipped approaches, with accountability for taking part.

Model 6 Blended Learning

Blended learning combines face-to-face instruction with online work, giving students some control over pace, path, or place. It is less a single method than a family of models, from station rotation to flex.

Explore TCEA's collected resources on blended learning.

Model 7 Flipped Learning

Flipped learning moves direct instruction out of the group space and into individual space, usually through short videos, so class time can be spent on application, discussion, and feedback. It pairs naturally with blended models.

See TCEA's resources on flipped learning.
