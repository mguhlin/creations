Part 3

Research: What the Evidence Says

"We need technology in every classroom and in every student and teacher's hand, because it is the pen and paper of our time, and it is the lens through which we experience much of our world." — David Warlick

Using technology for its own sake does not move learning. As Dr. Michael Cowling, a senior lecturer in educational technology, puts it: the technology we introduce must serve a purpose, and that purpose must be to enhance learning through improving pedagogy.

Source: How To Measure the Use of Technology in Teaching and Learning.

▸

A Growing Awareness

Research has raised hard questions about workforce readiness. One line of work argues that a generation once among the best educated in the world now ranks among the least well educated in the industrialized world, scoring near the bottom on international surveys of reading, numeracy, and problem solving.

The challenge described is stark: help students who leave school reading and calculating well below grade level graduate with much stronger skills, both cognitive and noncognitive, without spending substantially more than is spent now.

Source: Marc Tucker, Leading High-Performance School Systems.

▸

Is Technology a Time-Gobbler?

Research demonstrates the folly of our current priorities, such as investing heavily in technology when it has had, so far, such limited impact on student learning. — Dylan Wiliam

The argument is not that technology is bad. It is that faddish, time-consuming activities can crowd out the reading, writing, and thinking that actually build skill. The advice is to be willing to say "no, thank you" to work that looks engaging but does little.

What some studies have found

  • U.S. fourth-graders who report using tablets in nearly all of their classes have been found roughly a full year behind in reading compared with peers who report never using tablets.
  • Internationally, students reporting greater classroom technology use have tended to score worse on the PISA exam.
  • High levels of classroom technology use have correlated with lower student performance in several analyses.
  • One study found that more than a third of technology purchased by middle schools was never used, and only about five percent of purchases met the buyer's usage goals.

The Reboot Foundation concluded that technology is not always used in ways that prompt richer learning, and offered a few practical points:

  • Be deliberate about when and how technology is deployed.
  • Moderate use is often most effective for younger students.
  • For young children learning to read, non-digital tools often work better.
  • Tools that give immediate, instructional feedback can show real impact, and technology can especially help older students think more deeply.

Put simply, making sure every child reaches a baseline of proficiency in reading and mathematics does more to create equal opportunity in a digital world than expanding device access alone.

Source: OECD, Students, Computers and Learning: Making the Connection.

One Administrator's Perspective

A campus administrator described watching a fifty-minute middle school language arts lesson with the objective "learning to unpack the writing prompt." Students pulled tablets from a cart, logged in, and tried to remember how to use a note-taking app. For more than half the period, the only talk was about how to use the technology. Not one word touched the posted objective.

The problem is not always the technology. The same administrator watched an identical waste of time in another class where students struggled with scissors, folding, and pasting a paper foldable. In both rooms, if you had asked students what they were learning, they might have said they were learning to use a tablet, or to make a foldable.

The point is not that technology in the classroom is bad. It is that instruction built around the tool, rather than the objective, can interrupt learning and consume the time meant for it.

Why This Matters for the Campus Technology Specialist

On reading

  • Studies have long found that many children read for a surprisingly small share of the school day, even during long blocks labeled as reading instruction.
  • Roughly 15 extra minutes of reading per day can translate into several months of additional growth.
  • Modeling and demonstrating useful reading strategies has been observed less often than it should be.
  • When asked to demonstrate critical reading in writing, many students have struggled to provide details and arguments to support their interpretations.

On college readiness and writing

  • A large share of college-bound students have been found underprepared for college-level work, particularly in writing.
  • Substantial numbers of entering college students have needed remedial English.
  • Many students report doing little extended writing in history or social studies, and some graduate having never written a single long history paper.

The information age raises the literacy demands on everyone. Synthesizing and evaluating information from many sources is now a baseline expectation, which is exactly the kind of work that thoughtful technology integration can support, and that thoughtless integration can crowd out.

These points are drawn from Mike Schmoker's Results Now and Focus, citing Allington, Calkins, Haycock, and others. Figures are reported as cited in those works and reflect the period each study was conducted.

Are You Prepared?

Planning for educational technology today is different from yesteryear. The old focus was tools and silver-bullet solutions. Today, even with ample classroom technology, learning management systems, and a flood of creativity tools, many of these end up as inconsistent, one-shot efforts. There is, if anything, too much choice.

The question to be ready for: when a board member, principal, or parent asks "does technology make a difference here?", what evidence will you point to? The frameworks in the next section are built to help you answer.
