Copyright-Safe Curation

Newsletter AI, done with original words, clear links, and original visuals.

This deck is about a practical copyright approach for AI-assisted newsletter creation. The main point is simple: using generative AI to help curate and write a newsletter can be lower risk than republishing articles, but only if the workflow stays original, selective, and clearly linked to the source. I want the audience to leave with guardrails they can use the same day: summarize in their own words, add educator-focused commentary, avoid article images, and treat paywalled material with extra care. The deck starts with the risk frame, then moves into safer choices and a checklist.

Core idea

Lower risk does not mean risk-free

The safest newsletter does not replace the original source. It points readers back to it, then adds a practical educator lens.

  • Copied expression — reusing the article's words or structure.
  • Article images — the publisher's photos, charts, and screenshots.
  • Paywalled detail — summarizing a paid source so thoroughly no one needs it.
  • Close paraphrasing — same shape, lightly reworded.
✦ TCEA WebDeckCreator: Miguel Guhlinmguhlin.github.io/creations

This slide frames the whole conversation. AI-assisted newsletter creation is not the same as reposting someone else's work. It can be a safer pattern because the newsletter can point readers to the original article while adding a practical educator lens. But that safety depends on choices. If the newsletter copies too much expression, uses the publisher's image, or summarizes a paywalled article so thoroughly that readers do not need the original, the risk goes up. I would use this slide to move the audience away from a yes-or-no mindset and toward a workflow mindset.

Guardrail one

Summarize, link, and keep it limited

✍️Use original words

Write short summaries from your own understanding, not copied structure or phrasing.

🔗Send readers out

Make the linked source the place readers go for the full context.

⚖️Avoid magic numbers

There is no fixed safe word count or percentage for fair use.

Source: U.S. Copyright Office fair use FAQ

✦ TCEA WebDeckCreator: Miguel Guhlinmguhlin.github.io/creations

This is the first operational rule. Short, original summaries with links are usually the safer part of the newsletter workflow. The U.S. Copyright Office is clear that fair use can support limited use for purposes such as commentary, criticism, news reporting, teaching, and scholarly reporting, but it also says there is no fixed number of words or percentage that is automatically safe. That matters because many people want a rule like quote fifty words and you are fine. Copyright does not work that way. The practical move is to keep summaries short, add your own educator-focused interpretation, and make the source link do real work.

Guardrail two

Make the newsletter do new work

The issue should help educators understand why the source matters, not spare them from reading it.

Strong curation

  • Organized around a useful theme.
  • Adds a teaching connection.
  • Ends with a practical takeaway.

Weak curation

  • Repeats the article in a shorter package.
  • No new educator lens.
  • Drifts toward replacement.
✦ TCEA WebDeckCreator: Miguel Guhlinmguhlin.github.io/creations

This slide gets at the difference between curation and substitution. A strong issue is organized around a useful theme: assessment, instructional coaching, data privacy, classroom generative AI, or another educator-facing problem. It explains why the source matters, what to watch for, and how the idea might show up in practice. That is new work. A weak issue just compresses articles into convenient digests. That can drift into replacement, especially when the source is paid or subscriber-only. My recommendation is to make every item answer one extra question: what does this mean for an educator trying to make a decision?

Biggest risk

Article images are the danger zone

Watch this

Text summaries get most of the attention, but copied visuals often create the cleaner copyright problem.

  • Publisher images and article screenshots.
  • Stock photos embedded in posts.
  • Charts, graphics, and diagrams.
  • Safer: placeholders while drafting, then original or licensed visuals.

Source: Creative Commons attribution practices

✦ TCEA WebDeckCreator: Miguel Guhlinmguhlin.github.io/creations

This is the part I would emphasize most. People often worry about the written summary, then casually reuse the article image at the top of the newsletter. That can be a bigger problem. Images may be licensed separately, owned by a photographer, pulled from a stock library, or used by the publisher under terms that do not transfer to you. The safer workflow is simple: use placeholders while drafting, create your own feature image or visual summary, and use Creative Commons or public-domain media only when the license allows it. When using Creative Commons material, include the title, author, source, and license when that information is available.

Generated visuals

Useful, but ownership is nuanced

Good use

Create theme-based banners and simple visual summaries.

Human input

Protectable value may come from selection, editing, arrangement, and writing.

Tool terms

Check the image tool's usage terms before publishing.

Avoid

Do not imitate living artists, publisher styles, copyrighted characters, or logos.

Source: U.S. Copyright Office, Copyright and Artificial Intelligence

✦ TCEA WebDeckCreator: Miguel Guhlinmguhlin.github.io/creations

Generated images can reduce the need to copy publisher artwork. That is useful. The nuance is ownership. Current U.S. Copyright Office guidance centers copyright protection on human authorship, so a purely generated image may not receive the same protection as a human-authored work. In practice, that does not mean you cannot use generated images. It means you should be honest about what you can claim. Your human writing, editing, issue structure, selection, and arrangement may still carry creative value. I would also avoid prompts that imitate a publication's visual style or a living artist. The safer prompt is theme-based, original, and classroom-appropriate.

Common misconception

Attribution helps, it is not a shield

The point

Links and author names support transparency. They do not replace permission, a license, or a fair-use rationale.

  • Use attribution for reader trust and source discovery.
  • Use it for professional ethics and research trails.
  • Still keep the copied material limited.
✦ TCEA WebDeckCreator: Miguel Guhlinmguhlin.github.io/creations

This slide clears up a common misunderstanding. Attribution is good practice. It shows readers where the idea came from, helps them follow the source trail, and gives credit to the author or publisher. But attribution does not automatically make copying lawful. A newsletter can be perfectly attributed and still copy too much. That is why the workflow needs both attribution and restraint. The question is not only did I link to the author. The better question is did I use only what I needed, and did I add something meaningful for my readers. That is the safer habit.

Use extra caution

Public articles are not paid sources

Lower risk

Public articles, short original summaries, clear outbound links.

Higher risk

Paid newsletters, subscriber reports, database articles, books, and commercial PDFs.

Safer move

Briefly describe the source, link to the landing page, and avoid quote-heavy summaries.

✦ TCEA WebDeckCreator: Miguel Guhlinmguhlin.github.io/creations

Here I would make the distinction concrete. A public article with a short original summary and a clear link is one thing. A subscriber-only newsletter, a paid research report, a database article, or a book chapter is another. The more restricted the source, the more careful the newsletter should be. That does not mean you can never mention these sources. It means you should avoid turning them into a replacement. For paid or restricted material, I would keep the description lighter, avoid quotes unless they are essential, and link to the source landing page rather than reproducing the parts that carry the value.

This slide turns the guidance into a quick review tool. I would use it as the working checklist for each issue. Look at the text first: is the summary genuinely yours, or is it hugging the source too closely? Look at quotes next: are they short and necessary, or are they doing the work the summary should do? Then check visuals. If the image came from the article, pause. Finally, ask whether the issue adds practical educator value. If the answer is no, the newsletter is drifting toward republishing. If the answer is yes, the workflow is in safer territory.

The second half of the rubric checks what can be easy to miss after the writing looks finished. Images deserve special attention because a copied article image can create more risk than a short text summary. Accuracy also matters, especially for health, law, finance, student data, and product claims. The human authorship row is there because AI-assisted newsletter creation should still show human judgment: selecting sources, arranging the issue, editing the commentary, and deciding what belongs. If the issue reads like a pasted machine digest, the workflow is not doing enough editorial work yet.

This scoring guide keeps the rubric practical. The total score gives a quick read, but I would not let a high total hide a serious single-category problem. One copied image, one unsupported health claim, or one detailed replacement summary of a subscriber-only source can be enough to stop publication. That is why the no-go rule matters: any score of one means the issue pauses, and any score of two gets revised. This keeps the rubric from becoming a paperwork exercise. It turns it into a publishing gate that protects the writer, the organization, the sources, and the reader.

Project instructions

Build each issue for copyright-friendliness

Required workflow

  • Direct canonical links to original sources.
  • Short original summaries in a new structure.
  • Educator commentary and practical takeaways.
  • Verify factual claims against the linked source.
  • Score the issue with the rubric before publishing.

Hard limits

  • No article images unless licensed or permitted.
  • No close paraphrasing of paragraphs or structure.
  • Do not make a paywalled source unnecessary to visit.
  • No imitation of a style, artist, character, or logo.
  • Do not publish if any rubric category scores a 1.
✦ TCEA WebDeckCreator: Miguel Guhlinmguhlin.github.io/creations

This slide converts the rubric into project instructions. I would paste these into the newsletter project so they shape every issue before drafting begins. The first column tells the tool and the editor what the workflow should produce: direct source links, original summaries, educator commentary, checked claims, and a rubric score. The second column sets hard limits. Those are the boundaries that reduce copyright and trust risk. The most important one is the paywall rule. A newsletter can point to a restricted source, but it should not recreate the value people normally need a subscription or license to access.

Copy-ready language

Add a copyright note to each issue

This newsletter provides original commentary and summaries of publicly available sources for educational and informational purposes. Readers are encouraged to visit the linked sources for the full context. Article images are represented with placeholders unless separately licensed or created for this issue.

Use this as a starting point, then adjust it to match the actual sources and visuals used in the issue.

✦ TCEA WebDeckCreator: Miguel Guhlinmguhlin.github.io/creations

This is copy-ready wording for the bottom of each issue. It does not solve copyright by itself, and I would not present it as legal protection. Its value is practical. It tells readers what the newsletter is trying to do: provide original commentary, summarize public sources, and send readers back for full context. It also documents the image rule, which is where many newsletters get sloppy. I would adapt the note if the issue includes licensed images, Creative Commons images, or a paid source that is only described briefly. The note should match the issue, not pretend the workflow was safer than it was.

Bottom line

Use the safest pattern

Short original summary, plus practical educator commentary, plus a clear link, plus original or licensed visuals.

Curated

Organized around a useful issue theme.

Transformative

Adds interpretation and classroom relevance.

Attributed

Links readers to the original source.

Image-cautious

Avoids copied article artwork.

✦ TCEA WebDeckCreator: Miguel Guhlinmguhlin.github.io/creations

This closing slide gives the pattern I would want people to remember. AI-assisted newsletter creation is reasonably sound when it stays curated, transformative, attributed, and careful with images. The biggest risks are copied article images, excessive quoting, close paraphrasing, and detailed summaries of paid or subscriber-only content. The safest rhythm is a short original summary, followed by practical educator commentary, a clear link, and an original or licensed visual. That is easy to audit, easy to explain, and useful for readers. It also keeps the newsletter from becoming a substitute for the original work.
