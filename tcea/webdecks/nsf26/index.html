The big picture

A national push to make America AI-ready

NSF is funding a coordinated, state-by-state effort to accelerate AI readiness and adoption, reaching beyond K-16 education into businesses, public organizations, and individuals.

  • Goal: every community and business can participate in an AI-driven economy.
  • Approach: connect existing efforts, fill gaps, and scale what already works.
  • Structure: one coordinating Hub funded in every state, DC, and territory.
✦ TCEA WebDeck NSF 26-508 mguhlin.github.io/creations

Here is the core idea. The federal government wants the country to be ready for AI, and rather than build something brand new from scratch, NSF is funding coordination. The program reaches further than schools. It deliberately includes small businesses, local governments, and everyday individuals alongside education and workforce systems. The mechanism is one coordinating hub in every state and territory whose job is to connect the people already doing this work, find the gaps, and help the good efforts grow. The word I would underline is coordination. This is about being a connector and a backbone, not about delivering every training session yourself.

How the program is built

Three moving parts

①State Coordination Hubs

One per state or territory. Connects partners and scales AI readiness locally. This is the part Texas applies for.

②National Coordination Lead

Ties the hubs together, shares best practices, and informs national AI strategy. Funded separately.

③Catalyst Award Competitions

Topic-driven competitions that pilot and scale promising approaches over time.

✦ TCEA WebDeck NSF 26-508 mguhlin.github.io/creations

The program has three parts, and it helps to keep them separate in your head. The first is the State Coordination Hubs. That is the piece Texas would compete for, and the piece this whole conversation is about. The second is a single National Coordination Lead that sits above all the state hubs, helps them share what works, and feeds insights up into national strategy. That one is funded through a separate process. The third is a rolling series of Catalyst Award competitions that fund specific pilot projects over the life of the program. For our purposes today, only the first part matters. The other two are context.

Let me put numbers to it. Each state hub receives one million dollars a year for three years, with a possible fourth year if the hub can show a real need as it transitions to other funding. Up to fifty six hubs will be funded across the country, one for each state, the District of Columbia, and the territories. The award goes to a single lead organization, not to a loose coalition, and that lead is expected to have genuine statewide reach plus the operational muscle to manage a multi-partner effort. I share the dollar figure not because TCEA is chasing the money, but because it tells you how serious and how large this effort is.

On the federal side, this is not just NSF acting alone. NSF is the lead and runs the competition, drawing on three of its directorates. But three other agencies are formally attached. The Department of Labor brings the workforce system, including American Job Centers and registered apprenticeships. The Department of Agriculture, through its food and agriculture institute, extends the reach into rural and agricultural communities using the Cooperative Extension network that land grant universities run. And the Small Business Administration connects the hubs to small business support. The takeaway is that this program intends to touch the whole economy, not only classrooms, and it has the federal partners to back that ambition.

Devil's advocate

Why this could be the wrong call

Our name, their proposal

TCEA's reputation rides on a document we do not write or control. If the coalition is weak, that reflects on us.

No staff time rarely holds

Coordination generates meetings, reviews, and asks. The real cost tends to arrive after the letter is signed.

We may be the convener, not a guest

If TCEA wants to lead a statewide AI effort later, this could box us into a supporting role.

Likely no payoff for the effort

Only 10 hubs are funded in round one. If Texas is not selected, the tangible return may be zero.

Before I give my recommendation, let me argue the other side honestly, because this is not a free decision. Here is the case for saying no. First, we would be lending TCEA's name to a proposal we neither write nor control. If the coalition turns out to be thin, or the proposal underwhelms reviewers, that reflects on us, and we have no hand on the steering wheel. Second, I am skeptical of the phrase no staff time. Coordination work has a way of multiplying into meetings and requests once you are formally in. Third, and this is the one I weigh most, being a named partner in someone else's hub could quietly foreclose TCEA ever leading its own statewide AI effort. And fourth, only ten hubs are funded in the first round, so there is a real chance we spend reputation and goodwill for a proposal that simply does not get picked. None of these are disqualifying, but they are the honest reasons a thoughtful no would be defensible.

Bottom line

A low-risk yes, made deliberately

Real visibility and mission-aligned positioning at no near-term cost. The one risk worth weighing: a binding July commitment should be a deliberate choice now, not a surprise later.
